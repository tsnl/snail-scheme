# Village delivery game

Working description for a single-player browser game. The game, village and characters have yet to be named.

A teenage delivery boy has about a month before he leaves for university. His older brother, his only immediate family, has just been drafted. There are parcels to take up the valley, a small shaggy dog impatient to get going, and people who will still want to see him when his bag is empty. The war is going badly. For most of the month, what the boy can actually do about it is get on his bicycle and go see somebody.

**The things you can control can matter tremendously, even when much of life is beyond your control.** The brother's death, about a week in, holds that thought to its hardest test. What changes with the player's actions is who knows one another, who has what they need, what they can do for each other, and what the boy can carry with him when it is time to leave.

## A day on the bicycle

In the morning the player learns who is expecting a delivery and decides what else to take along. The work gives each ride a purpose. A conversation at the door can change the rest of the day. Someone mentions an item the boy could get from another resident, or recognizes something he could find for himself. Catching a fish means learning a place and leaving time to stop there. Remembering that someone wants it tomorrow gives the same stretch of river a different meaning on the ride home.

One **possible** early ride starts with a shop parcel for the farm. On the porch, one of the children asks what the pond looks like after rain. The boy can spend the long way home finding out. Granny mentioned wanting mushrooms, and he recognizes the turn for the shaded path. If he takes it, he might return with wet shoes, something for supper and a reason to visit two people tomorrow. Another player can ride straight to Main Street and catch a conversation while the shopkeeper closes. Each should feel like a day worth having.

- Let the player choose a route and leave enough daylight for curiosity on an ordinary day. Long journeys should have stretches where riding beside the dog is the whole pleasure.
- Requests can cross several households and several days. Establish a person's ordinary routine early enough for the player to notice when it changes. If a promise has a deadline, let the person explain why it matters.
- Branches can follow an action, a kept confidence, an introduction, or who actually receives something scarce. An occasional dilemma should leave two people with understandable, incompatible wishes.
- Main Street is an evening destination in its own right. People eat, share a beer, tell stories or quietly make room at a table. The boy can go there for company even when nobody has asked him for a thing.
- Bicycle upgrades and, eventually, access to a truck are candidates for progression. A rack that lets two awkward parcels travel together is a more interesting first upgrade than a numerical speed increase. The truck's ownership, cost and role in the ending remain open.

The strand-game reference is *Death Stranding*. Repeating a journey can change the relationship between the people at either end. One possibility is that, after the player introduces two residents, one begins making a delivery on their own. The player can see that consequence without opening a progress screen.

## His brother and his dog

The brother is the person the boy texts when he has no idea what to do. Give their exchange room for jokes, unfinished plans and questions from the brother too. A first-week message such as “did the dog ever get in the basket?” would give the boy something to tell him. It also makes their thread worth opening for reasons beyond the tutorial.

After roughly a week the brother dies. His death is fixed across the branches. Decide who tells the boy, where he is when he hears, and what happens the next time the player instinctively reaches for the phone. One proposed treatment keeps their old messages readable. The player can look up an earlier instruction in the brother's own words. For a new question, they have to decide whom in the village they trust enough to ask.

The dog is a companion with a life in this place. Pick a small shaggy silhouette the player can recognize against grass at dusk. Give the pair a ritual that belongs to them before the draft and survives it. For example, at one fork the dog always runs ahead, looks back, and waits to see which road the boy takes. Other possibilities include a resident who keeps a water bowl outside, or an evening after the news when the dog simply sits with him. How much help the dog gives with navigation or gathering is an open design choice.

## A village worth learning

An agricultural region in an invented JRPG world can have working farms, cell phones and a bullet train in the same view. The war draws working-age men away. Show who has taken on the work and how it is changing everyday life. The station can make distance felt from the start. A train crosses the valley in seconds while the boy is still pedalling the same hill.

This is a **first layout to discuss**. Two loops share Main Street and a river crossing. A player can learn the spokes of the village quickly and still have outside routes worth riding for their own sake.

| Place | People and daily life | What the ride offers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home lane and Main Street | The boy and the dog start near the shops. A convenience store, a place to eat, the doctor's clinic and the mayor's office give the street different hours of activity. | A familiar departure and return. The lit shop window can guide the last turn home. |
| Lower loop through the fields | The farmer and his young wife have several young children. The cheerful schoolmaster, with his big red-orange beard and hearty laugh, teaches near the path the children take. | The farm road carries large loads. A footbridge and fishing bank give the bicycle a reason to leave it. |
| Upper loop and wetland edge | A gruff old man lives where the lanes give way to swamp. Granny could live at the fork below him, within easy visiting distance but with a clear view of who rides past. | A longer, quiet ride with a raised causeway, a mushroom patch and a ridge overlooking the fields. |
| Station approach | The writer from the city might rent a place near the station. The boy's black-haired childhood best friend knows the local paths as well as he does. | The approach connects both loops. The bullet train platform is an ordinary place to visit long before departure day. |

The shopkeeper and doctor need lives away from their counters. Granny is a village elder, unrelated to the boy. The childhood friend has known him long enough to disagree with him and has decisions of her own to make about the end of the month. Romance is open. A question worth settling for the farmer and the schoolmaster is why these particular men are still here, and what the village expects of each.

## The last week

The calendar makes leaving imaginable from day one. The brother's death should have time to settle into ordinary days before the final week. We want a good or “true” ending, in the spirit of *Undertale*, for a player who helps everyone. Define “help” in terms of the people involved. Someone who asked for secrecy might feel betrayed by a parcel delivered exactly as promised.

Two ending ideas are on the table. In the **station ending**, the decision to board the university train can be small and personal. Seeing who can look after whom once the boy leaves is the payoff for a month of riding. In the **truck ending**, a credible local emergency brings the boy and village together for an evacuation. Earlier favors could determine who finds people, who brings supplies, who can drive, or who insists on going back for someone. The nature of the danger and whether the train still departs are open. In either version, write a complete ending for a player who formed only a few close ties.

## Picture, sound and the first screen

The strongest reference is the attention Studio Ghibli gives to ordinary acts and inhabited landscapes. The likely reference for children finding their way around a new country home is *My Neighbor Totoro*. *Tiny Glade* suggests how to make a shed, wall or garden carry a little history. *Breath of the Wild* and Spielberg's *Tintin* are references for expressive, readable figures in a stylized 3D world. Give faces, hands, the dog's movement and the way someone receives a parcel the same care as a broad valley view. [GKIDS synopsis](https://gkids.com/films/my-neighbor-totoro/)

Revisit one composition in different weather. At sunrise a rider and a fence cast long shadows across the same road. A sweltering afternoon can go nearly still except for insects. Rain erases the far ridge and brings out the roofline of the house up ahead. Night has deep blue skies, bright moonlight and an enormous moon, with enough light to ride by. Pokke Village from *Monster Hunter Freedom Unite* is the musical touchstone for the feeling of coming home. An original village theme could sound different from the ridge, at the table on Main Street, and in the week after the news. Make room for chains, tires, paws and weather to carry a scene on their own.

Opening the game already shows the village. The bike rests against a wall or on its kickstand, the boy is doing something appropriate nearby and the dog is with him. **Continue** eases the camera into play from that composition. For conversations, try a comic-style bubble close to the speaker against a visual-novel portrait treatment. A good comparison scene is a quiet exchange on a windy evening. Can the player read the expression and still feel present on the road?

## Questions for our next conversation

1. What does the brother ask the boy to do for **him** before he leaves? Is it something the boy can still do afterward?
2. What does the best friend want to happen to her own life at the end of the month? Which bit of the map would she show us first?
3. Does time advance while the player wanders, or with chosen activities and a deliberate return home? How much remembering should live in the player's head, and what would the boy plausibly write down?
4. What is something the dog would do entirely of its own accord that could change an ordinary day?
5. Is the final image the village seeing one boy off, or the boy helping a village get out? What would make that image ring true even for a player who missed someone?
